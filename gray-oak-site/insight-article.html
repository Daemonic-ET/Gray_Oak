When the Asset Management Plan Meets Operations

By Melody Quiroz Laven, Founder & Principal  ·  September 2026

A strategy can be perfectly aligned on paper and still disappear somewhere between the executive meeting and the maintenance crew. It happens more often than most organizations want to admit, and it rarely happens because anyone did something wrong. It happens because the plan and the work were never actually connected to begin with.

The Gap Between the Plan and the Floor

An asset management plan is, at its best, a statement of intent: these are the assets that matter most, this is the risk we're managing, and this is how we'll spend money and attention to keep things running. It's written by people who can see the whole system — budgets, risk registers, levels of service, regulatory obligations.

The work itself is carried out by people who can see one valve, one pump station, one work order at a time. Both views are correct. The trouble starts when nothing translates one into the other — when the plan describes intentions the crew never hears, and the crew's daily decisions never make their way back up to inform the plan.

Why Alignment Breaks Down

In our experience, it's rarely a single dramatic failure. It's usually a handful of small, ordinary gaps that compound over time: a risk framework that lives in a spreadsheet nobody outside of engineering opens, a levels-of-service target that was never translated into a maintenance priority, a work management system that captures what was done but not why it mattered, a planning cycle that runs on a different calendar than the budget cycle it's supposed to inform.

None of these is a crisis on its own. Together, they're enough to leave a technically sound plan sitting on a shelf while the organization runs on habit and tribal knowledge instead.

Line of sight isn't a document. It's a working habit — a plan that shows up in how decisions actually get made, at every level, not just the one where it was written.

What Line of Sight Actually Looks Like

The organizations that close this gap tend to do a few things consistently. They write plans in language the field can act on, not just language that satisfies a certification audit. They build a short, honest feedback loop so what crews learn on site changes what gets prioritized upstream, instead of information flowing in one direction only. And they revisit the plan often enough that it stays a living description of how decisions get made — not a document that's accurate the week it's approved and increasingly fictional after that.

None of this requires a bigger plan, or a more sophisticated one. It usually requires a smaller, clearer one, paired with the discipline to keep checking that it still matches what's actually happening on the ground.

Where to Start

If you're not sure whether your own plan and your own operations are actually connected, the fastest way to find out isn't a formal audit. Ask five people, at five different levels of the organization, to describe your top asset risk and what's being done about it. If the answers converge, you likely have real line of sight. If they diverge, that gap — not the plan itself — is usually the place to start.
